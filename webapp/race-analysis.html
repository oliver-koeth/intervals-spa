From result to intervention

Each chart maps to a known physiological signature. The report pairs an observable pattern with its likely mechanism and a concrete training response — the same logic coaches at Uphill Athlete, CTS and UESCA use to build durability, muscular endurance and eccentric resilience.

Durability · muscular endurance

Example: climbing fades late

Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Signature: Q4 dots sit higher (more HR) at the same uphill grade while graded pace slows — classic durability loss, not a VO₂max ceiling. The aerobic and muscular systems are no longer holding output once fatigue, micro-damage and glycogen depletion accumulate.
Train it: pre-fatigued climbing (2–3 h easy, then 30–60 min strong uphill), weighted vert hikes, back-to-back long days, and muscular-endurance hill repeats (e.g. 3×12 min uphill at Zone 3). Recheck fuelling — under-fuelling amplifies late fade.

Eccentric load · repeated-bout effect

Example: descending costs too much

Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Signature: HR (and metabolic cost) climbs on descents late in the race while descending pace stalls. This points to exercise-induced muscle damage — eccentric braking micro-tears the quads, so each stride recruits more fibres for the same speed.
Train it: build the repeated-bout effect with progressive downhill volume, eccentric strength (slow tempo squats/step-downs), plyometrics and technical descent repeats. Add a pacing rule to hold back on early descents so damage accrues later.

Climbing economy · VAM

Example: climbing power collapses on the steeps

Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Signature: ascending VAM (vertical metres/hour) holds on gentle grades but drops quarter-by-quarter on the steepest bands. Gentle-grade climbing is largely aerobic; steep-grade VAM exposes climbing-specific force and power-to-weight, which fail first under fatigue.
Train it: steep hill repeats and vertical-focused strength (uphill treadmill or power-hiking with load), plus economy work on race-relevant gradients. Pace to protect the hardest climbs for the back half — VAM at a fixed HR is your progress benchmark between blocks.

Aerobic decoupling · the durability number

Example: heart rate drifts away from pace

  • <5% · aerobically durable at this effort
  • 5–10% · moderate limitation
  • >10% · base, pacing or fuelling shortfall

The report now prints an Aerobic decoupling (GAP:HR) KPI — the drop in graded-pace-to-heart-rate efficiency from the first half to the second half. It is the single most practical durability number: rising HR at a stable graded pace signals plasma-volume loss, heat, dehydration or glycogen depletion eroding aerobic efficiency.
Train it: high-volume aerobic base, disciplined (slow) starts, earlier and more consistent fuelling/hydration, heat acclimation, and back-to-back long days. As you adapt, the same effort decouples later and by less.

Case studies: pattern-matching your race

Three common ways a race goes wrong look similar on a finish-line photo but very different in the report. Find the one that matches your data.

Case 1 · Metabolic / aerobic power limiter

Overpaced first half, then fades everywhere

Q1 felt easy and fast. From Q2 onward everything gets harder — climbs, flats and descents alike — and the fade never really stops. Nothing terrain-specific stands out; the whole race just slows down together with rising effort.

Report signalWhat you'd see
Aerobic decoupling (GAP:HR)High, often >10% — well past the healthy <5% band
Quarter averages — GAP/HR vs Q1Q2–Q4 all slower by a similar margin, regardless of terrain
Terrain-grade bandsPace loss spread evenly across climbs, flats and descents
Ascending VAM by slope segmentDrops across all bands together, not just the steepest
Stationary clustersUsually unremarkable — this is an output problem, not a stopping problem

Mechanism: starting above sustainable aerobic pace burns through glycogen and accumulates metabolic fatigue faster than the aerobic system can clear it. Because the whole body is above its sustainable intensity, the slowdown shows up everywhere — it is not a climbing- or descending-specific limiter.
Fix it: pace the first third to GAP, not raw speed; build aerobic threshold and long-run volume so the sustainable ceiling itself is higher; rehearse even/negative splits in training.

Case 2 · Muscular endurance & eccentric limiter — not aerobic

Even pacing, but the legs give out — up and down

Pacing was disciplined and HR never spiked. Yet by the second half, uphills feel like wading through mud and downhills hammer the quads — while heart rate barely moves. The cardiovascular system was never the bottleneck.

Report signalWhat you'd see
Aerobic decoupling (GAP:HR)Low, typically <5% — the aerobic engine held up fine
Quarter averages — HRNearly flat Q1→Q4; effort (by HR) looks the same all race
Ascending VAM by slope segmentQ4 collapses specifically on 15%+ bands; gentle bands barely change
Descending slope vs heart rateQ4 points sit at slower pace for the same HR as Q1 — not higher HR
Terrain-grade bandsEfficiency drops specifically on steep climbs and descents, not on flats

Mechanism: "the heart doesn't fail first" — the limiter is repeated force output in the climbing muscles and eccentric braking capacity in the quads, not oxygen delivery. HR stays low because the muscles simply cannot generate the force to raise it.
Fix it: muscular-endurance hill repeats and weighted vert hikes for climbing; progressive downhill volume and eccentric strength (slow-tempo squats, step-downs) to build the repeated-bout effect for descents.

Case 3 · Fuelling / GI distress — proxy signals only

Not enough calories in, or the gut stopped taking them

This one is genuinely harder to pin down from GPS and heart-rate data alone — under-fuelling and GI distress often overlap and can partially mask each other. Here is what to look for, and why it is not as clean-cut as Cases 1 and 2.

Report signalWhat you'd see
Stationary clustersSeveral short stops clustered together (bathroom breaks, lingering at aid) rather than one long planned stop
Terrain-grade bandsPace/GAP slows even on flat, easy-grade bands — not explained by terrain or durability
Aerobic decoupling (GAP:HR)Inconsistent — may look moderate rather than the sharp rise seen in Case 1
Quarter cadenceOften drops together with pace, out of proportion to the grade

Why it's messier: reduced splanchnic (gut) blood flow under high effort or heat is both a cause and an effect of GI distress, and it can blunt the heart-rate response instead of raising it the way Case 1's overpacing does. That means the classic "rising HR, falling pace" decoupling signature can be muted or absent even though something real is going wrong. GI symptoms are also extremely common in ultras — nausea alone is reported by the majority of finishers in some race-day surveys — so the report can only flag a suggestive pattern, not diagnose it.

Best current read: a broad, terrain-independent slowdown combined with clustered short stops is the strongest data-only hint of fuelling/GI trouble. Cross-check it against your own notes (nausea, bloating, missed gels/bottles) — the report cannot see what you ate or how your stomach felt.
Fix it: start fuelling earlier and in smaller, more frequent doses; train your gut with race-pace, race-nutrition long runs; keep a simple race-day nutrition/GI log so future reports can be cross-referenced against it.

Sources & further reading

The signatures above are not invented terminology — they come from coaches who train elite mountain/ultra athletes and from peer-reviewed exercise physiology. Every link below is publicly accessible.

Practitioners & coaches

  • Durability Training: Efficiency Factor and Decoupling for Endurance Athletes Uphill Athlete. Defines durability, efficiency factor and aerobic decoupling — the basis for the report's Aerobic decoupling (GAP:HR) KPI.
  • Muscular Endurance: All You Need to Know Scott Johnston, Evoke Endurance. Why repeated force output — not just aerobic power — decides late-race climbing performance.
  • Coaching the 2025 UTMB Champions: Scott Johnston's Focus on Muscular Endurance iRunFar. Applied case study of durability-focused coaching (Evoke Endurance) at the elite level.

Peer-reviewed research

  • Energy cost of walking and running at extreme uphill and downhill slopes Minetti et al., J Appl Physiol (2002). The grade-energy cost model the report's estimated GAP (grade-adjusted pace) is built on.
  • Downhill Running: What Are The Effects and How Can We Adapt? A Narrative Review Sports Medicine (2020), open access via PMC. Mechanisms of eccentric muscle damage and the repeated-bout effect used in the descending-slope signatures.
  • Exertional Rhabdomyolysis and Ultra-Trail Races: A Systematic Review Muscles, MDPI (2024), open access via PMC. Downhill-heavy elevation profiles cause disproportionate muscle-damage biomarkers vs. uphill/level running.
  • The Impact of Gastrointestinal Symptoms and Dermatological Injuries on Nutritional Intake and Hydration Status Open access. Evidence for how common GI distress is in multi-stage/24h ultras and how it disrupts fuelling — the basis for Case study 3's caveats.

Nutrition & gut training

  • Minimizing the Chance for GI Distress in Ultramarathons UESCA. Practical causes and prevention of exercise-associated GI distress, including reduced gut blood flow at high effort — referenced in Case study 3.

Benefits for athletes and coaches

Use the report as a post-race debrief template: what failed, where it failed, and what to train next.

1

Find fatigue signatures

Compare Q1–Q4 for aerobic decoupling, HR drift, and terrain-specific efficiency loss — the tell-tales of durability breakdown.

2

Separate terrain from fitness

Climbs, flats, and descents are analyzed separately, so a slow final quarter is explained by mechanism (eccentric damage vs. aerobic drift), not guessed.

3

Late uphill fade → durability & muscular endurance; steep-band VAM loss → climbing force; costly descents → eccentric strength and the repeated-bout effect; long stops → aid-station routines.

Your race data stays local.

Race Analysis runs in your browser. It does not create a server-side race database and does not upload GPX files to this site.

Privacy model

  • GPX uploads are parsed in the browser for the current analysis.
  • Cached activities and stream data live in your browser local storage.
  • No race analysis results are stored on the server side by Trail Data Hub.
  • You can clear local app data using browser tools or the app settings.
